First, your Google Sheet represents the starting point of the catalog. It already contains the basic identity of the products coming from your two main suppliers. That includes things like the garment name, supplier style code, base product information, some images, and maybe color and size information. This gives you the skeleton of the catalog, but it is still incomplete in two important ways. One part is customer-facing information that is missing, and the other part is internal production logic that has not yet been defined for each product.

The enrichment process therefore has two different goals happening at the same time.

The first goal is supplier enrichment. This means filling the gaps between what is currently in your sheet and what exists on the supplier websites. The supplier pages contain additional information that makes the product page complete and trustworthy. This includes things like the full image gallery, size charts, detailed descriptions, fabric composition, technical features, and sometimes spec sheets. That information exists, but it is fragmented across supplier sites instead of living inside your catalog. By bringing it into your sheet, every product record becomes complete and self-contained instead of depending on external pages.

The second goal is operational enrichment. This is the part you just added, and it is extremely important for a custom apparel business like yours. A blank garment is not automatically compatible with every decoration method. Some garments work well with embroidery, some with DTF, some with both, and some only allow certain placements or sizes. So for each product you need to define the decoration rules that determine how that garment can actually be sold on your site.

That means each product needs additional information that does not come from the supplier at all, but from your production knowledge and pricing model. For every garment you need to specify which decoration methods are allowed and where those decorations can be placed. For example, a product might allow embroidery on the left chest and sleeve but not on the back, while another garment might allow DTF on the full back but not embroidery because the fabric is too thin or stretchy. These rules become part of the product definition.

In addition to the allowed methods and locations, each product also needs the base sell price logic for the decoration variants you offer. Since your Shopify products are not simple blank garments but decorated garments, the product needs to know how pricing behaves depending on the decoration method. For example, the same hoodie may have a base selling price when decorated with embroidery on the chest, a different base price when printed with DTF, and potentially different prices again depending on placement or size of decoration. This information does not exist on the supplier side, so it has to be defined internally and attached to each product.

Once this layer exists, the sheet stops being just a supplier catalog and becomes a structured product configuration system. Each row no longer represents just a garment but a fully defined product object that contains three different categories of information. The first category is supplier data, which describes the physical garment itself. The second category is presentation data, which contains everything needed to build a complete and informative product page for customers. The third category is decoration and pricing logic, which defines how the garment behaves inside your custom storefront and production workflow.

At that point, adapting the data for Shopify becomes a translation step rather than a creation step. The enriched sheet already contains everything the product needs to exist in your store. The Shopify import simply converts that structured data into the format your store expects. Titles, descriptions, images, and size charts populate the visual product page, while metafields and metadata populate the internal logic that drives decoration options, allowed placements, and pricing behavior.

The final vision of your idea is essentially a centralized catalog engine for your business. Instead of your Shopify store being the place where products are manually built and edited, the store becomes the output of your structured product system. The Google Sheet holds the complete definition of every product, including supplier attributes, enriched content, decoration compatibility, allowed placements, and pricing logic. Shopify simply consumes that information and turns it into live products on the storefront.

When this works properly, adding a new garment to the catalog becomes much easier. You bring the base product into the sheet, enrich it with supplier information, define its decoration capabilities and pricing logic, and then the system can generate a fully functional Shopify product that behaves correctly inside your custom apparel workflow.
